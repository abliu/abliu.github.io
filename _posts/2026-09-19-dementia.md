# Approaches to dementia

Dementia affects millions of people worldwide (https://www.alz.org/alzheimers-dementia/facts-figures). My grandma suffered from dementia in her final years. My working conception of Alzheimer’s, the most common type of dementia, is a disease where plaques spread through the brain, neurons die, and neuronal connections are disrupted.

There seem to be a few broad approaches to reducing this burden:

## Prevention

The Lancet 2024 meta-analysis (https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(24)01296-0/fulltext) seems to be the latest canonical work on this. It names and ranks 14 modifiable factors that could be related to dementia. I’ve grouped them into four categories:

- Sensory loss/injury: hearing loss, vision loss, traumatic brain injury.
- Cardiovascular and metabolic issues: high LDL cholesterol, physical inactivity, diabetes, hypertension, obesity.
- Cognitive/psychological/social factors: social isolation, low education, depression.
- “Pollutants”: air pollution, smoking, alcohol.

How many cases of dementia are preventable by modifying these factors? These factors have been shown to be correlated with dementia but not necessarily causal, but the Lancet report says if these were causal, around 47% [<- AI check] of dementia cases could be prevented. I took this number with fistfuls of salt; the 47% came from a methodology I didn’t understand^[I understood the part where they estimated prevalences (e.g. “how common is hearing loss”) and relative risks (e.g. “how much more likely is dementia in people with hearing loss”), but they did a bunch of calculations beyond that which I can’t immediately justify.], plus we don’t know how causal these factors are and whether the field has looked at the right factors. But the upshot for me is that plausibly a decent fraction of cases are preventable, by doing things you would do anyway for your overall health^[Also the 14 factors are often beyond one’s control; some have genetic components and some may be inevitable in one’s environment.]:

- Sensory loss/injury: Get hearing aids for hearing loss^[describe ACHIEVE trial], be cautious in driving/biking/etc. to avoid brain injuries, etc.
- Cardiovascular and metabolic health: Eat well, exercise, etc.
- Cognitive/psychological/social factors and activities: Spend time with family/friends, be mentally active.
- “Pollutants”: Don’t overindulge in smoking/alcohol, etc.

Thus existing efforts on these fronts may also help reduce dementia down the road.

## Medicine

Alzheimer’s currently has no medical cure; the best results are a 25% slowing of cognitive decline after 18 months.^[Clinical studies measure this as follows: the control and treatment groups are given cognitive tests at baseline and after 18 months; the decline in scores in the treatment group is 25% less than in the control group.] A big bottleneck on progress is the slowness of clinical trials — they used to last 1-2 years (it takes a while for people with mild cognitive impairment to progress to fuller dementia) and the latest ones are 4-6 years as the field moves to treat people before they even show symptoms. The main thrust of Alzheimer’s treatment for the past three decades has been to neutralize and remove amyloid beta, a protein subunit of the aforementioned amyloid plaques which have been observed at higher levels in people with dementia:

<picture of amyloid plaques>

### Amyloid-targeting treatments

The last three decades have been a story of different ways of targeting antibodies (and initially, vaccines) at amyloid beta. ChatGPT wrote me a pretty engaging narrative <put in supplement> on this if you’re interested in the details, but the highlight for me is that these antibodies like lecanemab and donanemab managed to clear amyloid but still provided, at best, only the 25% clinical benefit shown above, and also risked side effects 10-25% of the time called amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) like swelling or bleeding in the brain. A potential reason for the modest clinical improvement is that the treatment needs to occur earlier: amyloid starts building up 5-10 years before cognitive decline and may already be damaging neurons and synapses pre-symptoms. This earlier treatment, plus some resolution to ARIA, is the current hope for amyloid hypothesis proponents and is the subject of ongoing clinical trials AHEAD 3-45 (ends in Nov 2027) and …

### Other

My friend Doug tells me that his mom mainly sees Alzheimer’s patients who still take the 70-year-old drug donepezil (which doesn’t target amyloid) instead of these new amyloid antibodies. I think it’s not surprising if the 25% slowing in reduction is no better than donepezil, and then the added risk of edema and hemorrhage tips the scale.

I find this a reminder that the cure for Alzheimer’s may not come from the amyloid hypothesis but could come from anywhere, especially because we often don’t understand how exactly these drugs work or the side effects (e.g. consider the seeming simplicity of “target amyloid” ~30 years ago, and how long that is taking to bear out). Doug’s bet is on making donepezil analogs.
